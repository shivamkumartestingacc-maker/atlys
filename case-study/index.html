Documentation · unpacking the brief

Reading the brief

“We should go somewhere” is intent with no owner, date or budget. “A concrete plan the group agrees on” has four visible parts (destination, dates, budget, itinerary) and one hidden part: agreement.

Travel products already help with the visible parts. Nobody owns agreement, so I treated it as the core problem.

What “agree” has to mean

Not a majority vote. If 3 of 5 want Bali and one can't afford it, the trip loses a person or quietly dies. My definition: every traveller confirms a plan that respects their hard limits. Preferences can lose; limits can't be ignored.

Reading the two signals

  • Agreement is a decision problem: inputs are uneven, mixed (can't-dos next to nice-to-haves) and conflicting.
  • Scattered talk is a memory problem: the information exists, but nobody can see the current state.

Questions I asked

  • Who starts the trip, and who carries it?
  • Do groups already know where? This led to two scenarios.
  • What does a group need to see to feel it has decided?
  • What makes someone say “I can't” early instead of going quiet?

Documentation · choosing the user

Organiser as entry, group as success

Designing only for the organiser produces an admin tool: one person plans, the rest are asked to “check the doc”. That fixes effort, not agreement. So the organiser starts the room, but a plan only counts when every member confirms.

The three roles are assumptions from desk research and personal experience, not interview findings. They're used to stress-test decisions.

Context I assumed

  • Groups of 4–8 friends who already chat daily.
  • Short leisure trips of 3–7 days, often short-haul international.
  • Uneven budgets and leave across the group.
  • The idea starts in chat, usually around a holiday.
04Research & insights

The tools groups use don't decide

No user access, so this is desk research and a review of today's tools. Each insight is tagged by the evidence behind it.

From briefDesk researchAssumptionHypothesis

  • Desk researchChat is built for talking, not deciding

    One timeline. Trip details compete with everything else and scroll away. Pinning saves a message, not a set of decisions.

  • Desk researchPolls answer one question at a time

    Fast, but isolated and majority-only. A poll can't tell “I can't do those dates” from “I'd prefer the beach”.

  • Desk researchPlanners assume the decision is made

    Docs and itinerary apps work once destination and dates exist, and usually only the organiser uses them.

  • AssumptionPeople avoid saying no in public

    Budget and leave are personal. Going quiet is easier than being the reason a plan fails.

  • HypothesisSeparating can't from prefer unblocks groups

    Once hard limits are visible and respected, preferences become easy to trade.

Documentation · research approach

What I did

  • Desk research on how groups organise trips: messenger threads, in-chat polls, shared docs, itinerary apps, expense splitters, and the hand-offs between them.
  • Tool review of the decision points in common messenger polls and collaborative itinerary apps.
  • Personal experience, used only to generate questions, never as evidence.

No interviews or surveys were run, so there are no participant quotes or statistics here.

With more time

  • Read 10–15 real trip threads (with consent) and map where they stalled.
  • Interview 6–8 organisers about the work between “we should go” and booking.
  • Interview people who dropped out of a group trip.
  • Inside a travel app: check how often several users from one group search the same destination.

Also observed

Assumption Momentum has a short half-life. If a group sees no visible progress in the first days, the idea fades, so speed to a first decision matters more than completeness.

05Problem framing

Groups struggle to turn shared travel intent into a plan because they have no structured way to combine preferences, resolve conflicting constraints and reach decisions everyone can commit to.

HMWhelp groups move from indecision to a mutually agreed trip plan?
Job to be done

When my friends and I say we should travel together, I want to see who's really in and what works for everyone, so we can lock a plan before the excitement fades.

Design brief
  1. Start where the group already is: the chat.
  2. Separate hard constraints from flexible preferences.
  3. Show conflicts openly. Keep minority views.
  4. AI drafts and suggests. People edit and decide.
  5. Nothing is final until every traveller confirms.

Opportunity

Bring the decision into the conversation: capture constraints and preferences as people talk, show where they clash, and give the group a clear path to a plan they all confirm.

Documentation · prioritisation

How I prioritised

Two questions per feature: does it move a group closer to agreement? and can I show it working? Both had to be yes. Features that only help after agreement (booking, itinerary, splitting costs) were cut, however useful.

Where the chat lives

Ideally inside the group's existing messenger. That isn't realistic here, so the prototype shows a Trip Room inside a travel app. The decision model doesn't depend on which chat it sits in; it could later run alongside an external messenger.

Documentation · interaction decisions

The AI asks before acting

Starting a plan sends a check-in to everyone, which is a social action. It has to be a person's tap, not the AI's. “Not now” is equally easy.

Answers stay hidden until all check in

Seeing the first answer anchors everyone else. Showing only who has answered keeps answers independent and gives the organiser a non-awkward reason to nudge.

Rule-breaking options stay visible

Hiding an option “someone” vetoed makes people guess who. Showing it, marked “over one private budget”, explains the trade-off without naming anyone.

Indigo means commit

In the design system, indigo is reserved for actions that commit: Start now, Submit, Confirm. Everything else is black, so commitment looks different.

Edit what the AI heard

Tap any line in the AI draft or any drafted constraint to correct it. Edited lines are marked “edited by you”, so the group knows a person checked it.

Chat + AI bot

Lightest to build, so I tried it first. But every decision becomes another message that scrolls away, which is exactly signal 02. A bot that reposts summaries becomes noise people mute.

Planner form

Perfect structure, but the work moves to one person's form. It solves admin, not agreement.

Poll voting

Kept its one-tap speed. Replaced majority with a consent vote, because a poll can't tell “I'd prefer not” from “I can't go”.

When the AI speaks in chat

After every message was too noisy; only when asked was too passive. Chosen: at milestones (intent detected, everyone checked in, plan proposed, plan confirmed).

Documentation · measurement plan

Why this North Star

It comes from the brief and measures the outcome, not activity. Messages or rooms can rise while trips still die. Requiring every “in” member to confirm stops one enthusiastic organiser from counting as a group decision.

How I'd measure it

  • Instrument each funnel step as an event with room ID and member count.
  • Baseline: groups planning in chat only. Proxy: several users from one group searching the same destination.
  • A/B test whether the AI offers to start a plan; compare the North Star at 30 days.
  • Interview groups that stalled at each step.

What would change course

High activation with low participation: the organiser is still doing it alone, so rethink the check-in. High participation with low decision completion: the options or the vote are the blocker. Low minority happiness: the losing view isn't being kept well enough.

12Learnings & next steps

What I learned, what's next

  1. 1
    Agreement isn't an average.Splitting can't-dos from nice-to-haves changed every screen after it.
  2. 2
    Design for the quiet member.Private budgets and a no-blame “can't” matter more than clever options.
  3. 3
    The AI works best as a clerk.Drafting and counting builds trust. Deciding would break it.

Limitations

  • No primary research
  • AI simulated with mock data
  • Prices and places are mock
  • Drop-outs and re-planning not designed

Validate first

  • Test with 3–5 real friend groups
  • Does the whole group check in?
  • Is the AI draft trusted?
  • Private vs public budget

Next

  • Re-planning when someone drops
  • Members add their own options
  • Nudges for missing check-ins
  • Hand-off to booking and visas
Documentation · open questions

Least sure about

Whether groups will move from their messenger into a new room. The decision model works the same alongside an existing chat, so if they won't move, the next version should meet them there.

Most likely to change

The 4 check-in questions and the consent vote. Both are easy to adjust: questions are data-driven and the vote rule is one function in the prototype.

Another learning

Show what was ruled out. Hidden options create suspicion; marked ones create understanding.
