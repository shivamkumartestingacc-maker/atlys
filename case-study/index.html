02The problem brief

Planning a trip with friends sounds exciting. Getting everyone to agree is a different story.

Agreeing on a destination, dates, budget and itinerary is hard. Conversations are scattered across group chats, preferences conflict, decisions get delayed, and trips often never happen.

Documentation · reading the brief

Two end points

“We should go somewhere” is intent with no owner, date or budget. “A concrete plan the group agrees on” has four visible parts (destination, dates, budget, itinerary) and one hidden part: agreement.

Travel products already help with the visible parts. Nobody owns agreement, so I treated it as the core problem.

What “agree” has to mean

Not a majority vote. If 3 of 5 want Bali and one can't afford it, the trip loses a person or quietly dies. My working definition: every traveller confirms a plan that respects their hard limits. Preferences can lose; limits can't be ignored.

03From brief to problem statement

Two signals, one problem

01

Getting everyone to agree

Groups struggle to align individual preferences and constraints, resolve conflicts and reach shared decisions.

02

Scattered conversations

Important discussions and decisions get lost across conversations, delaying progress and reducing momentum.

Problem statement

Groups struggle to turn shared travel intent into actionable trip plans because they lack a structured way to bring individual preferences together, resolve conflicting constraints and reach decisions everyone can commit to.

HMWhelp groups move from indecision to a mutually agreed trip plan?
Documentation · signals and framing iterations

Reading the two signals

  • Agreement is a decision problem: inputs are uneven (some loud, some silent), mixed (can't-dos next to nice-to-haves) and conflicting.
  • Scattered talk is a memory problem: the information exists, but nobody can see the current state. Silence gets read as agreement.

Both point to the same gap: a place to talk, but no shared place where decisions build up.

Framing iterations

VersionWhy I moved on
Help groups plan tripsToo broad. The brief is about agreement, not itineraries.
Help groups pick a destinationToo narrow. Groups who know where still fail on dates and money.
Help groups voteA mechanism, not a goal. Majority votes override hard limits.
Turn shared intent into an agreed planFinal. Covers both scenarios; names agreement as the outcome.

Documentation · choosing the user

Initiator as entry, group as success

Designing only for the initiator produces an admin tool: one person plans, the rest are asked to “check the doc”. That fixes effort, not agreement. So the initiator starts the trip, but a plan only counts when every member confirms.

Where the group dynamics come from

The five dynamics are assumptions from desk research and personal experience, not interview findings. Each one is a stress test: would the budget-conscious friend share their number here? Would the passive member answer this?

05Research & insights

Where group planning breaks down

Objective: understand where group trip planning breaks down, how people express preferences, and what stops conversations from becoming plans.

Insights to investigateHypothesisDesk research

  • HypothesisPlanning falls on one person

    Responsibility may fall disproportionately on the initiator, who then becomes the bottleneck.

  • HypothesisIndividual limits beat averages

    One member's hard budget or date limit may matter more than the group's average preference.

  • HypothesisTrade-offs must be visible first

    People may need to see what they gain and give up before they are willing to agree.

  • Desk researchA shortlist alone doesn't decide

    Destination lists and polls narrow choices, but they don't handle conflicting constraints, so indecision remains.

NoteNo primary research was run. These are hypotheses and desk-research observations, not validated findings.

Documentation · research approach

What I did

  • Desk research on how groups organise trips today: messenger threads, in-chat polls, shared docs, itinerary apps, expense splitters, and the hand-offs between them.
  • Tool review of the decision points in messenger polls and collaborative itinerary apps.
  • Personal experience, used only to generate questions, never as evidence.

There are no participant quotes or statistics on this page.

How I'd validate

  • Read 10–15 real trip threads (with consent) and map where they stalled.
  • Interview 6–8 initiators about the work between “we should go” and booking.
  • Interview people who dropped out of a group trip.
  • Inside a travel app: how often several users from one group search the same destination.

  2. Make everyone's preferences visible.
  3. Avoid overlooking individual constraints.
  4. Give the group clarity on what has been decided.

Documentation · why these four

From needs to opportunities

Each opportunity answers a need: Capture stops limits getting lost in chat; Align makes everyone's preferences visible; Decide keeps individual constraints from being overruled; Act gives the group clarity on what is settled.

The participant's job

When I'm invited on a group trip, I want to say what I can't do without being the one who blocks it, so the plan works for me too. This second job led to private budgets, a no-blame “can't do it” vote and explicit confirmation.

07Questions & assumptions

What I asked, what I assumed

Questions
  • Why do group decisions get delayed?
  • How do people handle conflicting budgets and preferences?
  • What makes someone commit to a trip?
  • When should AI intervene?
  • Does everyone need to agree on every aspect of the trip?
Assumptions
  • One or two members carry most of the planning.
  • Individual budget limits matter more than group averages.
  • People need clarity before committing.
  • AI can reduce coordination effort by structuring information.
  • Groups progress more when decisions are visible.

ValidationDesk research, available external data and targeted hypothesis testing.

Does everyone need to agree on everything?

My answer: no. Everyone must agree on the things that can exclude them (dates, budget, destination). Lower-stakes items like activities can stay open in the workspace.

08Scope & constraints

Reach the decision. Skip the booking engine.

What we're solving

  • Bringing group travel conversations together
  • Capturing individual preferences and constraints
  • Identifying conflicts and possible trade-offs
  • Helping groups compare options and decide
  • Converting decisions into a shared trip plan

What we're not solving

  • Flight and hotel bookings
  • Payments and expense splitting
  • Real-time travel assistance
  • Complete travel management
  • Post-trip experiences
Scope principle

Focus on helping groups reach a decision, rather than building another end-to-end travel booking platform.

Documentation · prioritisation

How I prioritised

Two questions per feature: does it move a group closer to agreement? and can I show it working? Both had to be yes. Features that only help after agreement were cut, however useful.

Constraints

Time-boxed assignment, no user access, mock data and simulated AI. The prototype is a mobile UI shown on desktop. It lives as a Trip Room inside a travel app; the decision model could later run alongside an external messenger.

When the AI speaks in chat

After every message was too noisy; only when asked was too passive. It speaks at milestones: intent spotted, everyone checked in, plan proposed, plan confirmed.

What the AI never does

Confirm for anyone, hide a minority view, pick the winner, reveal a private budget, or present a draft as final.

Documentation · interaction decisions

The AI asks before acting

Starting a plan sends a check-in to everyone, a social action. It has to be a person's tap. “Not now” is equally easy.

Answers stay hidden until all check in

Seeing the first answer anchors everyone else. Showing only who has answered keeps answers independent and gives the initiator a non-awkward reason to nudge.

Rule-breaking options stay visible

Hiding an option “someone” vetoed makes people guess who. Showing it, marked “over one private budget”, explains the trade-off without naming anyone.

Indigo means commit

In the design system, indigo is reserved for committing actions like Confirm. Everything else is black, so commitment looks different.

13Growth strategy

Every trip recruits the next organiser

Growth hypothesis

Group participation can naturally drive adoption, because planning requires multiple members to contribute.

  2. 02They invite friends to participate.

  4. 04The group reaches a shared decision.

OpportunityEvery trip introduces the product to a new group of potential organisers and participants.

Documentation · growth risks

Why it could work

A group trip can't be planned alone: every member has to answer the check-in. Each invite is a product exposure with a clear reason to join, not a marketing message.

What could break the loop

  • Invitees answer once and never return, so they never become organisers.
  • Groups stay in their existing messenger and only one person uses the app.
  • A bad first trip experience stops the next one.

Member-to-organiser conversion in section 14 tests this directly.

Measurement principle

Measure whether groups progress from travel intent to an agreed plan, not how many messages or recommendations the product generates.

Guardrails

  • Drop-off right after conflicts are shown.
  • AI draft edit rate (high = low trust or poor extraction).
  • “Happy with this plan” among people who voted for another option.

How I'd measure it

Instrument each step as an event with group ID and size. A/B test whether the AI offers to start a plan, and compare the North Star at 30 days. Interview groups that stalled at each step.

15Learnings & next steps

What I learned, what's next

  1. 1
    It's a coordination problem.Group travel planning is fundamentally about coordination and decision-making.
  2. 2
    Averages hide people.Individual constraints should never be hidden by group averages.
  3. 3
    AI should facilitate, not just generate.It adds more value helping groups decide than producing recommendations.
  4. 4
    Decisions must stay visible.Shared decisions need to remain transparent and editable.
Next steps
  1. 01Validate the decision-making model with real groups.
  2. 02Test whether AI-generated preference summaries are accurate.
  3. 03Evaluate whether the experience reduces time to agreement.
  4. 04Measure conversion from trip intent to confirmed plans.
  5. 05Refine the product based on observed group behaviour.
Documentation · limitations

Limitations

  • No primary research; insights are hypotheses and desk research.
  • AI behaviour is simulated with mock data.
  • Prices and destinations are mock content.
  • Drop-outs and re-planning are not designed.

Most likely to change

The 4 check-in questions and the consent vote. Both are easy to adjust: questions are data-driven and the vote rule is one function in the prototype.
