02 The problem

“We should go somewhere” is easy to say. Getting everyone to agree on where, when and how much is where trips die.

Conversations scatter across group chats, preferences conflict and decisions slip. The brief: get a group from a vague idea to a concrete plan they all agree on.

Documentation · unpacking the brief

Reading the brief word by word

The brief describes two end points. “We should go somewhere” is intent with no commitment: no owner, no date, no budget. “A concrete trip plan the group agrees on” has four parts: a destination, dates, a budget and an itinerary, plus one hidden part, which is agreement.

Most travel products already help with destination, dates and itinerary. Agreement is the part nobody owns. So I treated agreement, not planning, as the core problem.

What “agrees” has to mean

Agreement isn't a majority vote. If 3 of 5 want Bali but one person can't afford it, the trip either loses a person or quietly dies. I defined agreement as: every traveller has confirmed a plan that respects their hard constraints. Preferences can lose; constraints can't be ignored.

How I read the two signals

  • Getting everyone to agree is a decision problem: inputs are uneven (some people are vocal, some silent), mixed (can't-dos sit next to nice-to-haves) and conflicting.
  • Scattered conversations is a memory problem: the information exists, but nobody can see the current state. Silence gets read as agreement.

Both point to the same gap: the group has a place to talk but no shared place where decisions accumulate.

Questions I asked of the brief

  • Who starts the trip, and who carries it?
  • Do groups already know where they want to go? (Led to the two scenarios.)
  • What does a group need to see to feel it has decided?
  • What would make someone say “I can't” early instead of disappearing?
03 The user & moment

A group of friends, one of them carrying it

The primary user is the organiser: the friend who says “let's actually do this”. But the product only works if the rest of the group takes part, so every design choice is tested against the quietest member.

Primary · assumptionThe organiser

Starts the plan, nudges people, ends up doing the spreadsheet. Wants momentum without being the bad guy.

Participant · assumptionThe enthusiast

Replies fast with big ideas. Easy to mistake their excitement for the group's.

Participant · assumptionThe constrained one

Has a real limit on leave or money. Often goes quiet rather than be the one who says no.

Documentation · choosing the user

Why the organiser, and why not only the organiser

Group-travel tools often default to designing for one planner who does everything. That fixes the organiser's admin, but not agreement, because the rest of the group stays outside the tool. I chose the organiser as the entry point (they start the room) and the whole group as the success condition (the plan is only real when every member confirms).

The three roles above are assumptions from desk research and my own experience of group trips, not interview findings. They're there to stress-test decisions: “Would the constrained one say ‘I can't’ here?”

Context I assumed

  • Groups of 4–8 friends who already chat daily in a messenger.
  • Short leisure trips (3–7 days), often domestic or short-haul international.
  • Uneven budgets and leave across the group.
  • The idea starts in chat, usually around a holiday or long weekend.

Each of these is listed again in Assumptions & questions.

04 Research & insights

What the tools people use don't do

With no access to users, I ran desk research on how groups plan today and reviewed the tools they use. Every insight below is labelled by how much evidence sits behind it.

From briefDesk researchAssumptionHypothesis to test
Desk research

Chat is built for talking, not deciding

Group messengers keep a single timeline. Trip details compete with everything else and scroll away. Pinning helps one message, not a set of decisions.

Desk research

Polls answer one question at a time

In-chat polls are quick, but each one is isolated and majority-based. They can't hold “I can't do those dates” next to “I'd prefer the beach”.

Desk research

Planning tools assume the decision is made

Shared docs and itinerary apps are good once a destination and dates exist. They don't help a group get there, and usually only the organiser uses them.

Assumption

People avoid saying no in public

Budget and leave limits are personal. It's easier to go quiet than to be the reason a plan fails.

Assumption

Momentum has a short half-life

If a group doesn't make visible progress in the first days, the idea fades. Speed to a first decision matters more than completeness.

Hypothesis

Separating can't from prefer unblocks groups

Once hard limits are visible and respected, preferences become easier to trade, because nobody fears being overruled on what matters.

Documentation · research approach

What I did

  • Desk research: how group trips are usually organised (messenger threads, in-chat polls, shared docs or sheets, itinerary apps, expense splitters) and where each hands off to the next.
  • Tool review: walked through the decision points in common messenger polls and collaborative itinerary apps, noting what each captures and what it drops.
  • Personal and observed experience: used only to generate questions, never as evidence.

I didn't run interviews or surveys for this assignment, so there are no participant quotes or statistics on this page. Anything that would need them is marked as an assumption or hypothesis.

What I would do with more time

  • Chat archaeology: with consent, read 10–15 real trip threads and map where they stalled.
  • Organiser interviews (6–8) on the work they do between “we should go” and booking.
  • Silent-member interviews: people who dropped out of a group trip, and why.
  • Product data (if inside a travel app): how often multiple users from the same group search the same destination.
05 Problem framing

Groups struggle to turn shared travel intent into actionable plans because they lack a structured way to bring individual preferences together, resolve conflicting constraints and reach decisions everyone can commit to.

How might we help groups move from indecision to a mutually agreed trip plan?
Job to be done · organiser

When my friends and I say we should travel together, I want to quickly see who's really in and what works for everyone, so we can lock a plan before the excitement fades.

Job to be done · participant

When I'm invited on a group trip, I want to say what I can't do without being the one who blocks it, so the plan works for me too.

Opportunity

Bring the decision into the conversation. Capture constraints and preferences as people talk, show where they clash, and give the group a clear path to a plan they all confirm.

Design brief
  • Start where the group already is: the chat.
  • Separate hard constraints from flexible preferences.
  • Show conflicts and trade-offs openly; keep minority views.
  • AI drafts and suggests; people edit and decide.
  • A plan is tentative until every traveller confirms.
Documentation · how the framing changed
VersionFramingWhy I moved on
v1“Help groups plan trips together.”Too broad. Planning tools exist; the brief is about getting to agreement, not itineraries.
v2“Help groups choose a destination.”Too narrow. Groups who already know where still fail on dates and money (Scenario A).
v3“Help groups vote on trip options.”Voting is a mechanism, not the goal. Majority votes can override someone's hard limit.
v4 · finalTurn shared intent into an agreed plan by structuring preferences, resolving constraints and reaching decisions everyone commits to.Covers both scenarios, names agreement as the outcome, and leaves room for the mechanism to be designed.

Opportunities I considered

  • Reduce the organiser's admin (chosen as a side effect, not the goal).
  • Make it safe to say no early (chosen: private constraints, veto vote).
  • Turn chat into structured data automatically (chosen: AI drafts, people confirm).
  • Inspire destinations (deprioritised: helps Scenario B only, and isn't the bottleneck).

Why two JTBDs

One job for the organiser would have produced a planning tool. Adding the participant's job forced features that protect the quieter members: private budgets, a “can't do it” option that blocks without blame, and explicit confirmation.

07 Scope & constraints

Solve the decision. Leave the booking.

The bottleneck is agreement, not booking. So the work stops the moment the group confirms a plan.

In scope
  • Spotting trip intent in chatWhere the idea already lives.
  • Collecting constraints and preferencesPrivate, short, separate from the noise.
  • Surfacing conflicts and trade-offsThe step groups skip today.
  • Comparing viable optionsBy fit per person, not popularity.
  • Group decision and confirmationThe definition of done.
Deliberately out of scope
  • Booking, payments, visasDownstream. A natural next step, not the bottleneck.
  • Day-by-day itineraryPlanning detail after agreement.

Documentation · prioritisation

How I prioritised

For each candidate feature I asked two questions: does it move a group closer to agreement? and can I show it working in the prototype? Features that scored on both made the cut. Features that only helped after agreement (booking, itinerary, splitting costs) were cut, however useful.

Where the chat lives

The ideal is to work inside the group's existing messenger. That isn't realistic for this assignment, so the prototype shows a Trip Room: a group chat with a trip plan tab, inside a travel app. The decision model doesn't depend on which chat it sits in; it could later run as a bot or companion to an external messenger.

If groups can share preferences, see and resolve conflicts, and decide together in one place, more trip conversations will turn into confirmed plans.

Documentation · interaction decisions

Why the AI asks before acting

The intent card waits for a tap on “Start a trip plan”. Starting a plan sends a check-in to everyone, which is a social action. That has to be a person's choice, not the AI's. “Not now” is equally easy.

Why answers are hidden until everyone checks in

If people see the first answer, they anchor on it. Showing only who has answered (not what) keeps answers independent, and gives the organiser a clear, non-awkward reason to nudge.

Why options aren't hidden when they break a rule

Hiding an option that “someone” vetoed makes people guess who. Showing it, marked “over one private budget”, explains the trade-off without naming the person.

Why the confirm button is indigo

In the design system, indigo is reserved for actions that commit: Start now, Submit, Confirm. Everything else is black, so the moment of commitment looks different.

Documentation · why the others were rejected

Chat + AI bot

It's the lightest to build and needs no new screens, which is why I tried it first. But every decision becomes another message that scrolls away, which is the exact problem in signal 02. A bot that keeps reposting summaries becomes noise people mute.

Planner form

Gives perfect structure. In practice it moves the work from the chat to the organiser's form: one person fills it in, others are asked to “check the doc”. It solves admin, not agreement.

Poll-based voting

Familiar and fast, so I kept its speed: the decision step is one tap per option. I replaced the majority rule with a consent vote, because a poll can't tell “I'd prefer not” from “I can't go”.

Where the AI speaks in chat

I explored the AI posting after every relevant message (too noisy), only when asked (too passive) and at milestones (intent detected, everyone checked in, plan proposed, plan confirmed). Milestones were chosen: four messages per trip, each one useful.

Documentation · measurement plan

Why this North Star

It comes straight from the brief and measures the outcome, not activity. Messages sent or rooms created can rise while trips still die. Requiring every “in” member to confirm stops one enthusiastic organiser from counting as a group decision.

How I'd measure it

  • Instrument each funnel step as an event with room ID and member count.
  • Baseline: group trips planned in chat only. Proxy: multiple users from one group searching the same destination.
  • Test: A/B on whether the AI offers to start a plan, then compare the North Star at 30 days.
  • Read qualitatively alongside: interview groups whose plans stalled at each step.

What would make me change course

High activation with low participation means the organiser is still doing it alone; rethink the check-in. High participation with low decision completion means the options or the vote are the blocker. Low happiness among people who lost the vote means the minority view isn't being kept well enough.

12 Learnings & next steps

What I learned, what I'd test next

Key learnings · from the design process
  • Agreement isn't an average. Splitting can't-dos from nice-to-haves changed every screen after it.
  • Design for the quiet member. Private budgets and a no-blame “can't” matter more than clever options.
  • The AI is most useful as a clerk. Drafting and counting builds trust; deciding would break it.
  • Show what was ruled out. Hidden options create suspicion; marked ones create understanding.
Limitations
  • No primary research. Users and insights are assumptions and desk research.
  • The AI is simulated with mock data. Real intent detection will make mistakes.
  • Prices and destinations are mock content.
  • Only the core journey is prototyped. Re-planning when someone drops out isn't designed.
Validate first
  • Prototype test with 3–5 real friend groups, using their own chat
  • Will the whole group check in?
  • Is the AI draft trusted and corrected?
  • Private vs public budget
Next
  • Re-planning when someone drops out
  • Any member can add an option
  • Smart nudges for missing check-ins
  • Hand-off to booking and visas
Later
  • Work inside external messengers
  • Shared itinerary after confirmation
  • Cost splitting
  • Learn a group's patterns across trips
Documentation · open questions to take into testing

What I'm least sure about

Whether groups will move from their messenger into a new room at all. The decision model works the same as a companion to an existing chat, so if they won't move, the next version should meet them where they are rather than ask them to switch.

What I'd change with real data

The 4 check-in questions and the consent vote are the two pieces most likely to change. Both are easy to adjust: questions are data-driven, and the vote rule (veto on “can't”) is one function in the prototype.
